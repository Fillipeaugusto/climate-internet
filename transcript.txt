Latin America is facing a growing climate challenge, extreme heat, long periods without
rain, wildfires, intense rainfall, floods, and other extreme events.
But there is another problem that receives less attention.
Many of the places where the environmental data matters the most are rural or poorly connected.
And a sensor can detect the problem.
But if there is no reliable way to communicate that information, the warning may arrive too
late for a solution.
Once a wildfire starts, it can spread extremely quickly destroying vegetation and habitats
while it relays in large, almost off-CU2, and other pollutants into the atmosphere.
Our proposal is a climate internet, a low-cost distributed communication network designed
for climate monitoring and environmental resilience.
The network is built on a long-range, low-power radio technology such as LoRa or open mesh
network ecosystems, instead of requiring every device to reach each other on the internet,
we use NERBY nodes that can relay small packages of information from one point to another.
A message can travel across the network until it reaches the gateway or a connected station
or another person in the match.
This makes the system especially useful in regions where cellular coverage is weak or
expensive or unavailable in a situation of chaos.
The physical network is only part of the solution, and for that we are not reinventing the radio.
We use commercially available LoRa hardware from manufacturers like Rec Wireless or Realtek
that already have solved the long radio communication.
Our work is focused on the software, the platform that brings this network to life.
It provides a visual layer where the user can register the nodes, locate them on the map,
monitor their status and understand how the mesh is connected.
The system can prioritize critical environment alerts and help identify alternatives to communication
pathways when one node becomes unavailable.
For our initial prototype, we simulate environmental data to demonstrate how an alert moves through
the mesh from a remote node to the platform.
But beyond the software, the point where we truly innovate is in the scalability model.
Instead of scaling by developing more infrastructure ourselves, we scale through collaboration.
We join the network as a registered collaborators.
We call them environmental guardians.
A school, a farmer, an NGO, or a municipality can each be added to the platform, a sentinel
node, and configured with exactly the sensors their location needs.
A school might monitor air quality, a farmer, a soil moisture, or a rainfall.
Every node sensor sets its independence.
The same infrastructure adapts to each collaborator's problem.
And this benefits both sides, because on the one hand, the collaborators help authorities
to make better decisions and faster environmental decisions.
And on the other side, by joining the network, they are also gaining something for themselves.
A resilient communication line on their own, Laura can carry basic text messages.
Even when the cellular networks are down, so in an emergency, being part of the mesh isn't
just a contribution, it's a safety net.
Our goal is not to replace existing environmental monitoring systems, cellular networks, or public
authorities.
It's to add a resilient communication layer, where those systems do not reach, or when
they stop working.
Climate internet connects sensor, people, and environmental information, so that critical
data can travel farther, earlier, and more reliably.
Because climate resilience is not only about collecting data, it's about making sure the
right information can reach the right people when it matters.
The climate is changing, the way we connect environmental information needs to change too.
One sensor can collect data, one node can transmit it, and a network can make it useful.
